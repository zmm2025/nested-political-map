How it works

Every state gets a color, and neighboring states get very different ones. Each county gets its own shade of the state's color, chosen so that neighbors contrast. Each town or township gets a shade near its county's.

The shades are balanced. Weighted by area and mixed as light, a county's towns average to that county's color, and a state's counties average to that state's color. When the pieces get too small to see, what is left is the parent's color.

Choosing the colors

There are six state colors: red, amber, green, teal, blue and violet. Each has its own lightness as well as its own hue, so neighbors stay apart for people with red-green color blindness (). County and town colors come from an optimizer working in OKLab. It pushes neighbors apart while holding each state's hue, the range a screen can show, and the averages.

Why towns and not cities

The Census Bureau's county subdivisions do nest perfectly. In the Northeast and Midwest they are real towns and townships. Elsewhere many are areas drawn for statistics, so some names may look unfamiliar, and a large city can be split across several.

Notes

  • Borders are the 2025 Census cartographic boundary files at 1:500,000, so Connecticut shows its nine planning regions.
  • The lower 48 states and Hawaii share one equal-area scale. So do Guam and the Northern Marianas. Alaska is at 35%. Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands and American Samoa are at 150%. Hawaii's remote northwestern atolls are left out.
  • City blocks, tracts and other finer Census areas are not included.
